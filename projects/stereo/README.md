# Stereo

One small simulated bike-share city, seen as five synchronised layers: what really happened, what got logged, what the pipeline did, what the dashboard shows and what a forecasting model learned. Page: `projects/stereo/index.html`. Model: `model.js` (browser and Node). Tests: `tests/model.test.js`. The card preview draws `preview-data.js`, which `tools/make-preview-data.js` writes from `model.js` (re-run it after any change to the model; the tests fail if it is stale). The stage’s faces are Space Grotesk and Space Mono (SIL Open Font License 1.1), self-hosted from Fontsource in `fonts/` with the licence in `fonts/OFL.txt`.

## What it’s for

Data work asks you to hold two pictures at once: the number on the screen and the process beneath it. Wilfrid Sellars called the first the manifest image and the second the scientific image, and asked for a stereoscopic view that fuses them, a way to see how things hang together. Here the dashboard is the manifest image; the world, the log and the pipeline are the scientific image. A visitor breaks one layer and watches the fault rise to the layers above it (and never below), or starts from an odd dashboard number and digs down to its cause. They should leave able to trace a number down to its cause and a fault up to its consequences, and knowing that a dashboard can move because the city moved, because the data broke, or because the ruler changed.

### What is on the page

- **Cause → effect.** Each layer has a menu of changes. Pick one; a pulse rises from that layer through every layer it reaches. Each layer is drawn twice, like the two images of an anaglyph: in red, as that layer records it, and in cyan, as it would be if nothing had gone wrong. The two are added (canvas ‘lighter’ compositing) on a dark field, so where they agree they fuse into one white line, each drawn a fraction of a pixel to its own side so a half-pixel red and cyan fringe stays at the edges (a little more on screens without high density, which cannot draw half a pixel crisply); where they part, the truth is dashed and the gap between them hatched in both colours, so the difference never rests on colour alone. A tick box (drawn as a pair of red and cyan glasses) hides the cyan image, which is how real systems look. The details panel for each layer shows that day: a map of bikes and riders (world), the raw event stream in UTC (log), every pipeline step with rows in and out (pipeline), the three tiles and their definitions (dashboard), and what the model learned (model).
- **Reading the stack.** The five strips share one 60-day axis; the slider’s thumb sits over the cursor line that runs down every strip, and dragging along any strip moves it too. Where a layer’s record parts from its truth, its lines split into red and cyan and the layer’s edge, status mark and menu go out of register the same way (a fault); where the world itself moved, the lines stay fused and thicken on those days (a real change). The world layer is the cyan image alone: it is the truth; the weather beside it is a fainter fused pair. The log and the pipeline each also have a row of bars, ‘not as it was’: per night, the events (log) or output rows (pipeline) that differ from the truth, so a fault that keeps the counts the same, such as the time-zone bug, still shows its gap at its own layer. On the model strip the dots are the actual rides. A strip of the card mount between the dashboard and the pipeline marks the line between what appears and the process beneath. The pulse is a line that climbs from the changed layer, at the day it changed, through every layer it reaches, and stops at the last one; choosing a change first scrolls the stack into view so the pulse is seen from its start. On a laptop the timeline and all five layers fit on one screen (about 600 px tall at 1366 px wide); on a phone they stack. Each state has an address to share: `#fault=firmware`, `#case=3`.
- **The look.** The stage is a stereograph card: a buff mount (#d8c9a3) with rounded corners and a printed rule, holding a slate window (#20242a) whose top edge is cut as the classic twin arch, one arch per eye. Layer names are stamped in Space Mono capitals on tongues of the mount that reach into the window, and a layer whose record has parted from the truth has its name printed out of register. The modes, the suggestions, the cases and the answers are square tabs of the same card; the chosen one carries its rule printed twice, red and cyan, offset. On the mount (paper) the two inks print out of register; in the window (light) they add up to white. The card preview draws the same card on its canvas, with the names on the mount beside the window.
- **Detective.** Six tickets of rising difficulty, each from a hidden cause. The truth line is hidden, so every layer is the red image alone. You start at the dashboard, examine other layers, read the checks each one offers, and name the layer and the cause. Then the truth is drawn, the layers that were right fuse into white, and the cause is explained. One ticket is a real change: nothing is broken.

## How I know

**What is modelled.** Sixty days (Mon 2 June to Thu 31 July) in an invented city of eight stations, with a ninth, Riverside, that can open on 7 July.

- *World.* Each day has a temperature and a chance of rain (27%). Ride requests are Poisson (drawn exactly for hours with a mean below 30, by a rounded normal approximation for busier hours): on a dry 20°C weekday about 600, multiplied by exp(0.032 × (temperature − 20) − 0.48 × rain − 0.14 × weekend). Requests are spread over the hours with a weekday double peak (8 am and 5:30 pm) or a weekend afternoon hump, and over stations by morning, evening and leisure weights: homes to work in the morning, back in the evening, the waterfront at weekends. Every request is an event in a time-ordered simulation with real bikes: if the station is empty the rider is turned away; otherwise a bike leaves and docks at the destination after a trip whose length depends on distance, purpose and a log-normal spread with a few long leisure rides. Overnight a truck shares out the bikes docked at midnight to each station’s target, in a fixed order (any extra go to Harbour). Bikes still out on a ride are missed and dock later, so the last station on its round usually starts a few bikes short: with the default seed, Hillside on 59 of 60 days, about 3 bikes short on average. A mechanic checks five bikes a day. With the default seed: 28,947 rides, 7.9% of requests turned away, mean trip 17.4 minutes, median 14.2.
- *Log.* Checkouts and ‘no bike’ events come from the rider’s phone; returns come from the dock. Each event carries the sender’s UTC timestamp and the server’s arrival time, and lands in a nightly partition by arrival. About 4% of events are resent by flaky networks (normal).
- *Pipeline.* Real transforms on arrays of rows, run over the whole season at once and counted per nightly partition: read; filter out maintenance; dedupe on (type, ride id), keeping the first copy; convert UTC to local time (UTC − 7 h) and derive date and hour; pair each checkout with its return on ride id (a checkout with no return is dropped, and duplicated keys fan out, as a real join does); inner join to a stations table; group by date.
- *Dashboard.* Rides per day, riders turned away per day, and average trip length (the mean of durations).
- *Model.* A Poisson regression of daily rides on (temperature − 20)/5, rain and weekend, fitted by Newton’s method (with a ridge of 1 on the slopes so a dry month still has a solution), refitted every night on the pipeline’s last 28 days, and used to forecast the next day from that day’s weather.

**The faults.** Each begins on a set day and is applied in its own layer only; everything above is recomputed from the changed data.

| Layer | Change | What it does (default seed, from 30 June) |
|---|---|---|
| World | Heatwave, 10–16 July | 30–33°C, no rain. Rides 712 a day against 440 without it; riders turned away 124 against 37. Signal: every layer matches its truth. |
| World | Riverside opens, 7 July | 18 more bikes and riders of its own. Signal. |
| Log | Dock firmware at Harbour loses 30% of returns | Those trips cannot be paired and are dropped: rides 6.9% low; the retrained model forecasts 4.7% low. |
| Log | App sends checkouts and ‘no bike’ twice for 35% of riders | The correct dedupe removes every copy. Reaches the pipeline’s insides; nothing above it moves. |
| Log | Rail Station’s dock clock an hour ahead | Every trip ending there looks an hour longer: average trip 38% high. Rides untouched, so the model does not move. |
| Pipeline | Stations table not updated for Riverside | The inner join drops every row that starts there: rides 8% low, riders turned away 25% low. |
| Pipeline | Date taken from the UTC timestamp | Rides after 5 pm local are filed under the next day. The first faulty day loses its evening; afterwards each day carries the evening before. The pipeline loses no trips, but the season’s last evening (120 trips) is filed under 1 August, past the dashboard’s range. The counts per night are unchanged, so the pipeline’s ‘not as it was’ bars carry the gap. The model’s error against the dashboard goes from 4.2% to 10.8%. |
| Pipeline | Dedupe on event id instead of ride id | The normal 4% of resends leak through and the pairing join fans out: rides 8% high. |
| Dashboard | Average trip length redefined as the median | 18.6% lower overnight. Nothing beneath changed. |
| Model | Training query joins the previous day’s weather | The model learns that rain changes rides by −1% (trained on the truth: −35%) and its error rises from 4.2% to 23%. |

When a lower fault reaches the model, the page says plainly that the model has learned the fault as if it were the city, and shows that, judged against its own (corrupted) data, its error can look better than it is against what really happened.

**What I checked, and how.** The model lives in its own file and is tested headlessly: `node projects/stereo/tests/model.test.js` (41 checks, under a minute). Every run in the tests is computed from scratch, without the cache the page uses.

- *No faults, exact truth.* For six worlds (default, heatwave, new station, both, two other seeds), the dashboard’s rides, riders turned away and average trip length equal the world’s own counts exactly, day by day, with no tolerance; every true ride appears exactly once in the pipeline’s output with its true date, start and duration. The healthy filter removes exactly the maintenance events and the dedupe exactly the resends.
- *Faults stay in their lane.* For each of the eight faults, every layer below the fault’s layer is byte-for-byte identical to the fault-free run, its own layer differs, and the page’s comparison never reports a lower layer as affected. Each fault’s signature is checked exactly: the firmware fault removes 27–33% of Harbour returns and the dashboard loses exactly the rides whose return vanished, on the day each started; the double-send copies are all app events and the pipeline output is identical; every restamped event is a Rail Station return moved by exactly 3,600 s; the stations-table fault removes exactly the Riverside rides and requests; the time-zone fault moves exactly the trips after 17:00 local in faulty partitions by one day, loses none in the pipeline, and the dashboard misses exactly the season’s last evening; the definition change shows exactly the median; the weather-join fault leaves earlier nights’ coefficients identical and at least doubles the forecast error. World changes are checked to move the dashboard while every layer still matches its truth. The pipeline’s per-night ‘not as it was’ count adds up to its missing, extra, misdated and retimed rows, is zero for faults above it and for the absorbed double-send, and is non-zero on every faulty night of the time-zone fault although that fault leaves the per-night counts unchanged. Only the menu’s own faults are accepted (an address such as `#fault=constructor` is ignored). The overnight truck conserves bikes: those docked at midnight plus those still out equal the fleet, every day.
- *The model is really fitted.* Every nightly fit satisfies the Poisson score equations to within one millionth of the total rides, every forecast equals exp(x·β) for the stored coefficients, every training target is the pipeline’s own daily count, and the error figures are recomputed from the forecasts. On synthetic Poisson data with known coefficients, the fitter recovers them to within 0.03.
- *Determinism.* The same seed gives identical layers; another seed gives another city; the page’s cache gives exactly what a fresh computation gives. All randomness is a hash of (seed, purpose, ids), so switching a fault on never shifts any other random draw. The card preview’s stored series (`preview-data.js`) equal a fresh computation, so the card stays computed rather than typed in, without simulating the city twice on every card load.
- *Each detective case can be solved.* For each ticket the test checks that the claim in the ticket holds on the data it describes (for example, that rides really came in below the forecast on at least 11 of 14 days), that the checks on the answer’s own layer flag something, and that the set of flagged checks differs from what every other answer on the menu would produce in the same world, so the evidence the visitor can reach singles out exactly one answer. The real-change ticket flags nothing outside the weather record. Ticket numbers are computed from the run, never typed in, and say what they are (ticket 1 quotes the weekly averages before and after the change); the seeds were chosen so that the ticket’s claim holds (weather can otherwise swamp a 7% effect).

In the browser I checked the page at 1366 × 768, 1280 × 800, 960 × 700 and 375 × 812 (no horizontal scroll; on a phone one grouped menu replaces the five per-layer menus). At 1366 × 768 the timeline and all five layers take 596 px, with every layer’s heading on one line for all ten changes; on the phone they take 707 px, and choosing a change scrolls them into view. I also checked the card preview at 320 × 200 and on `projects.html` (it requests only its own files, including `preview-data.js`), every change’s status line, and a detective case through to the verdict.

An independent check found an error my first round of testing missed: in a visible tab, the strips’ ResizeObserver reports on the first rendered frame, before the city is computed, and the redraw threw a TypeError. My browser pane rendered no frames, so it never fired there. Drawing now waits until the city exists. I re-tested by loading a copy of the page whose ResizeObserver reports 16 ms after it starts watching, and once with it reporting at once, and by clicking Play, the truth switch, a layer and a strip before the city was built: no errors. The console stayed empty on the page, in every change, and in the card (the projects page’s only 404s come from other projects’ cards). The pane I used renders frames only when a screenshot is taken, so I checked the smooth scroll by where it ended (all five layers on screen), and did not watch the pulse animation in this round; its drawing code is unchanged, and it now starts after the scroll.

**What is simplified.** The city is invented and small. Requests do not respond to empty stations by walking to the next one. Docks never fill. The overnight rebalancing is unlogged, and the truck simply misses bikes still out at midnight. The forecast uses the next day’s actual weather as if the weather forecast were perfect. The pipeline is run over the whole season at once with per-night partitions, rather than night by night with late-arriving data. Each fault is a clean switch on a given day; real faults drift, overlap and come and go. The model forecasts rides that were served, not demand, so its weather effects are weaker than the true ones (rain −35% against −38%, +5°C +11% against +17%) because full stations cap the busiest days. And the most important simplification, which the page states: only a simulation has a truth line. A real system never gets one, which is why the detective mode hides it.

**What would change the conclusion.** The sizes of the effects depend on choices: Harbour receives about a quarter of all trips, so a 30% return loss there costs about 7% of rides; at a quiet station it would barely register. Some faults would hide in noise without the shaded truth line, which is why the tickets lean on the forecast or on one sharp day. The uniqueness of each case holds for the menu on the page; a larger menu (two faults at once, a fault that mimics a real change) could make two causes leave the same evidence, and then no amount of drilling would separate them without new checks.
