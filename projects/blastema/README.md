# Blastema

A flatworm made of cells that regenerates from local rules, and whose voltage memory can be rewritten to grow a two-headed worm. Page: `projects/blastema/index.html`. Model: `model.js` (browser and Node). Tests: `tests/model.test.js` (run with `node tests/model.test.js`); a larger survey of cuts: `tests/survey.js`.

This file keeps the full record. The page itself carries only a short paragraph and in-stage captions.

## What it’s for

To show that the goal of a body’s shape can be held by a crowd of cells rather than stored as a picture for them to copy. Cut the worm and each piece finds its way back to a whole, smaller worm from local readings alone. Then rewrite one remembered value in the cells, and the same cells with the same rules build a different body. The goal is collective, and it can be edited.

## How I know

### What each cell does

Every cell carries the same small rule book, a little like a genome: how wide the body should be at each fraction of its length, and where head, pharynx and tail begin. What no cell holds is a picture in place: where the head is, how long the body is, or which cell should be which. Each cell works those out from its own state and its six neighbours, many times a second, by following the same few rules.

1. **Listen and pass it on.** Two organiser cells, one at the head and one at the tail, send out a signal. Every cell hears how many cells away the nearest organisers are, by taking its neighbours’ numbers and adding one.
2. **Work out where you are.** From those two distances alone a cell can tell its place along the body (a ratio, so it holds at any size), how far it is from the midline, and how long the body is. That decides whether it is head, eyespot, trunk, pharynx or tail.
3. **Keep the shape.** A cell at the edge that finds itself outside the width its reading allows dies off. A gap that reads as inside is filled by a neighbour dividing. This is how a stump turns into a smaller whole worm.
4. **Notice a wound.** A cell that suddenly loses neighbours knows it is at a cut. It compares its remembered place with its neighbours’ to tell which way the cut faces, and buds out new cells: the blastema. In the bud a new organiser appears, a head if the cut faces head-ward and a tail if it faces tail-ward, unless the body already has one. A piece whose cut faces neither way, such as a lengthwise half, grows no bud; once it has healed, if it still hears no head, the edge cell that remembers being furthest head-ward becomes one (and likewise for a tail).
5. **Remember.** Each cell also holds a voltage value, shared with its neighbours through their junctions. It decides what a tail-facing cut should build. Normally that is a tail. Rewritten, it is a head.

The rewrite brush changes only that remembered value. In this model it is a two-state switch shared by the whole body, not a map: a normal worm reads the same everywhere, and a touch of the brush anywhere on a worm tips the cells under it, which tip their neighbours, until the whole body reads head-like, some 3 to 9 seconds later at normal speed. Where you touch makes no difference to the result. (A spot of only a cell or two would be pulled back by its neighbours, but the brush is wider than that.)

The body does not change when you paint. It changes the next time it has to rebuild, and the value is copied into every new cell, so it lasts through later cuts. Nothing in the cells’ rules was edited: the change is held in the cells’ state, not in their rule book.

**What is modelled.** Cells sit on a hexagonal grid, about 3,300 of them on a wide screen, 2,250 on a medium one and 1,450 on a phone. Every step, each cell computes its next state from itself and its six neighbours and nothing else: the two nearest organisers it hears of (counted in hops, passed from cell to cell), a relayed estimate of body length, a wound alarm, a remembered place and distance from the midline, and the voltage memory. Its identity and its edge behaviour (divide into a gap, die back, or bud) follow from those readings, as listed above. Every cell also carries the same fixed rule book: a short table of how wide the body should be at each fraction of its length, and where the head, eyespots, pharynx and tail begin. That table is the worm’s outline written in proportions, so the shape is not worked out from nothing. What is not stored anywhere is the outline in place: no cell knows where the head is, how long the body is, or what it should be, until it reads its neighbours. A cell keeps itself if the sum of its two organiser distances, minus the body length, is within the width the table allows for its place along the body; with this grid’s way of counting hops, that sum works out to the cell’s distance from the midline. Everything is read as a fraction of the current length, which is why a piece rescales instead of regrowing the missing half at full size.

The voltage memory is a value in each cell, coupled to its neighbours and pulled towards one of two states: 0, normal, and 1, head-like. With the threshold I chose it behaves as a switch for the whole body, not a map of it. A normal worm holds 0 in every cell, so the Voltage lens shows it as one even colour; the lens draws exactly the stored value. Any patch of 1 wider than a cell or two tips its neighbours, and the head-like state spreads until the whole body holds it. A two-headed worm grows because the cells at a tail-facing cut read 1 there; which end you touched makes no difference.

**What is simplified.** Almost everything. Real planarians regenerate from dedicated stem cells (neoblasts) and use many signalling pathways; here, any edge cell can divide. The pole signals are hop counts relayed between neighbours, not diffusing molecules, because diffusion across a hundred cells would take far too many steps to watch. The voltage here is one number per cell with a simple rule, and one state for the whole body. Real bioelectric patterns differ from place to place, arise from ion channels and gap junctions, and are only partly understood. In the experiments of Durant and colleagues the edited state was also stochastic: recut worms came back two-headed only part of the time. Here, once the memory is head-like, every later cut that leaves a tail-facing wound grows a head. Real flatworms crawl; this one only sways. Pieces are pushed apart by a fixed distance when cut, and the camera zooms to keep them in view, and moves closer to the first finished two-headed worm; none of that is part of the cells’ rules. Cell division takes turns in a fixed three-colour pattern across the grid, so that two separate pieces regrowing towards each other can never fill touching gaps in the same instant and fuse. The first worm is laid out once using the same reading the cells use, then left to them. In this model a piece shorter than about a fifth of the original worm cannot make two organisers far enough apart, so it fails and fades, and pieces between a quarter and two-fifths of the worm sometimes fail too (numbers below). Real planarian fragments below a certain size also fail, though they do far better than this: Morgan reported pieces about 1/279 of a worm regenerating.

**What I checked, and how.** The model lives in its own file and is tested in Node (`projects/blastema/tests/model.test.js`). Locality: for 650 cells and empty sites taken mid-regeneration, the test fills every site more than one hop away with random values and confirms the update comes out identical; a second test checks that the update code makes no global look-ups. Regeneration: 20 random cuts at the three worm sizes the page uses, anywhere from a fifth to four-fifths along the body and tilted up to 35 degrees. Each piece is chosen at the moment of the cut and followed by the cells it started with, so a piece that fades counts as a failure. Every piece at least two-fifths of the original length (28 of them) came back with exactly one head organiser and one tail organiser, a head (with eyespots) of 12 to 32 per cent of its length, a tail of 10 to 30 per cent, a pharynx centred 42 to 68 per cent of the way from the head, and a length-to-width ratio between 2.8 and 6.5; so did the 12 pieces of a quarter to two-fifths in that run. A larger batch (`tests/survey.js`, 90 cuts tilted up to 45 degrees) is less flattering. All 126 pieces at least two-fifths long came back right. Of 39 pieces between a quarter and two-fifths, 30 did: 5 faded, 2 came back stubbier than the tolerance and 2 with a single eyespot cell. Of 15 smaller pieces, from 0.15 of the worm up to a quarter, 7 did. Pieces were patterned in a median of 800 steps, about 7 seconds at 1×, and the slowest in 1,850 steps, about 15 seconds; reshaping goes on for several seconds more. A piece cut at 40 per cent kept the uncut worm’s length-to-width ratio within 30 per cent. Split lengthwise, each half regrew its missing side and came back with one head and one tail (5 of 5). A sliver from the tail tip does not become a worm and fades. Rewrite: an untouched worm holds a memory of exactly 0 in every cell, before and after a cut. One touch of the page’s brush, at either end or in the middle, tipped the whole body within 1,050 steps at every size. After one touch, the next cut gave a two-headed worm with its pharynx in the middle, cutting that worm again gave two-headed pieces again, and cutting the normal-looking tail piece, which carries the rewrite, gave a two-headed front half (4 of 4 runs). Without a rewrite, no cut gave two heads. A spot of one or two cells is pulled back to 0. Rougher handling is less tidy: several quick cuts made while pieces are still rebuilding can leave a smaller misshapen piece, stubby or with two tails.

**What would change the conclusion.** The point is narrow: local rules, a shared rule book and a few remembered values are enough to rebuild a whole, rescaled body and to keep an edited target. It does not show that planarians do it this way. The proportions are set by the width table and a few thresholds that I chose; different choices give a different worm, but not a different principle. A model where cells copied a stored outline in place would also regenerate, so the tests that matter are the locality checks, and the fact that pieces become smaller wholes rather than the missing half.
