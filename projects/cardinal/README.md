# Cardinal

A real query optimiser (DuckDB, compiled to WebAssembly) guesses how many rows each step of a join will produce. The page shows its guesses beside the real counts, lets you skew and correlate the data until the guesses break, and then shows that a filter widened on top of a bad guess makes the plan flip to a slower one.

## What it’s for

An optimiser does not look at your data when it plans a query; it looks at a small model of your data (row counts, distinct counts, smallest and largest values) and assumes the rest: that keys are equally likely, that filters are independent. A visitor should leave having seen that model at work at every operator, having broken it twice (correlation, skew), and having watched the consequence: a plan chosen on a wrong guess that runs measurably slower than the plan the data called for. The gap between the model and the data is where slow queries come from.

## How I know

### What runs

- **Engine.** `@duckdb/duckdb-wasm` **1.32.0**, an exact pinned stable release published on npm on 2025-12-16 (checked against the npm registry on 2026-11-02), which bundles **DuckDB v1.4.3**. It is loaded from jsDelivr with the package’s documented `getJsDelivrBundles()` / `selectBundle()` route and a Blob-URL worker, so it works on a static host. `engine.js` refuses to run if the loaded module reports any other version. It is loaded only on the project page, never in preview mode, and only after the page asks for it (about 6 MB compressed; on a Save-Data or 2G connection the page waits for a click).
- **Counts.** Every number in the plan comes from `EXPLAIN (ANALYZE, FORMAT JSON)`: the estimate is the operator’s `extra_info["Estimated Cardinality"]`, the real count is `operator_cardinality`. `model.js` parses that JSON (`parseProfile`), collapses pass-through projections, and computes the q-error as max(est/act, act/est) with counts below 1 treated as 1 (Moerkotte, Neumann and Steidl 2009). Nothing in the plan view is hard-coded.
- **Plan changes.** A hash join’s first child is the probe side and its second the build side, as in DuckDB’s profiler output. `describeChange` compares two plans for the same tables: a different set of intermediate joins is a join-order change; the same joins with a different build input is a build-side flip. The sentence on the page is generated from that comparison.
- **Timing.** Wall-clock time of the plain query (no profiling), measured from the page through the worker, as the median of five runs after one unmeasured warm-up. Browser timing is noisy (JIT tier-up, garbage collection, other tabs); the page says so and shows the range. The “other build side” time is measured by switching off DuckDB’s `build_side_probe_side` optimiser and writing the join the other way round; the scripted check confirms from that plan’s own profile that the build side really is the other one.
- **Data.** Generated in the browser with SQL from `generate_series` and a seeded `hash(i, stream, 1979)`, so every visit gets identical rows (a hash of the row number rather than `setseed`/`random()`, so the result cannot depend on threading). Each stream is reduced modulo the prime 1,000,003 before any small modulus: I found that DuckDB’s multi-argument `hash()` keeps the low bits of different streams related (`hash(i, 21, s) % 4` fixes `hash(i, 22, s) % 4`), which would have made “independent” columns secretly linked. 1,000,000 orders, 20,000 customers, 1,008 products on a laptop; 400,000 orders and 8,000 customers on phones. Customers live in 60 cities, five to a province (12 provinces); products fall in 12 categories.
  - **Skew** θ: each foreign key is drawn as `floor(n · u^(1/(1−θ)))`, so P(id = k) = ((k+1)/n)^(1−θ) − (k/n)^(1−θ), a Zipf-like law with exponent θ that puts the most weight on the lowest ids. Customer 0 and product 0 are therefore the heaviest, and city 0 and province 0 hold the heaviest customers. The page shows the measured share of the busiest customer and of product 0.
  - **Province–category link** ρ: with probability ρ an order’s product is moved to the same rank inside the category that matches the customer’s province. With uniform marginals this makes Cramér’s V between province and category exactly ρ (proved in the tests). The share of orders in the home category is then ρ + (1 − ρ)/12, not ρ, because the orders that are not moved still land there one time in twelve (81.7% at ρ = 0.8). The page measures both V and that share from the generated orders and shows them; the control is labelled as the share *moved*.
  - **Customer filter** (the selectivity control): one city (1/60 of customers), one province (1/12) or the whole country.
- **Read-only SQL box.** `validateSql` allows a single SELECT/WITH statement and refuses DDL/DML, settings, file-reading functions, nested SQL and file-like string literals; independently, the page runs `SET enable_external_access = false` after building the tables. Keywords are matched only in keyword positions (not inside quoted identifiers, not as an alias after AS, not next to a dot), quoted function names are checked unquoted, and WITH RECURSIVE is refused. Before running a free-form query the page asks DuckDB for its estimates and refuses plans with cross products or more than 50 million estimated rows anywhere. Those estimates are the very thing the page shows to be unreliable (a self-join of orders on customer at skew 0.8 is estimated at 11 million rows and really gives over 100 million), so they are only a first filter: a free-form query runs once for its profile and once or twice for time (not five times), each run is sent as a pending query with a 5-second wall-clock limit and a Stop button, and both cancel it through DuckDB-Wasm’s `cancelSent()`. Checked in the browser: a query that would run longer is stopped after 5.1 s and the page carries on; Stop and moving to a guided step both stop it within a second or so. If DuckDB ever fails to stop within 2.5 s of a cancel, the page ends the worker and starts a fresh engine (the tables are generated again); that path is written but I could not provoke it, since every cancel I tried landed.

### What I found, and how the steps changed because of it

The spec asked for a textbook path. DuckDB 1.4.3 does not behave like the textbook everywhere, so the steps were built around what it actually does:

1. **Correlated filters.** For two equality filters on one table (city = 7 AND province = 1) DuckDB does not multiply selectivities: it keeps the most selective one. Correlated columns are then estimated well and *independent* ones are overestimated (recorded in `steps.json`: 371 guessed, 348 real when province follows city; 371 guessed, 28 real when it is independent). The underestimate the spec asked for does happen across a join, where DuckDB treats the two tables’ filters as independent, so step 2 correlates customer province with product category and filters on both (×14 too few at ρ = 0.8). The step says the single-table behaviour in one line.
2. **The plan flip.** DuckDB’s statistics cannot see skew, so turning the skew dial never changes its plan; it only makes the same plan wrong. A flip that the visitor can cause and that is driven by a bad estimate does exist: in the best-seller query (orders for product 0, joined to customers), DuckDB compares its guess for product 0’s orders (about 800, from the distinct count) with its guess for the filtered customers. With one city (about 370) it builds the hash table on customers; with one province (about 1,500) it flips and builds on the orders. Widening the filter causes the flip; skew is what makes the flipped plan a bad one. The page, the card and the change sentence (which now names the filter change as the cause) say it that way round. Under skew 0.8 those orders are really about 250,000 rows, so the flipped plan builds a large hash table: 31.9 ms against 12.2 ms for the other build side in the recorded run (2.6 times slower; 13.2 against 5.7 ms at the phone size), while at skew 0 the same flip is harmless. That is a genuine flip produced by an estimate crossing a threshold, and the step is honest about what triggers it.
3. **Skewed join key.** At skew 0.8 the busiest customer (id 0, who lives in city 0) places about 14% of all orders, while the top ten place only about 22% and it takes about 625 customers (3%) to reach half. City 0 holds about 15% of orders, almost all of it customer 0’s: without that one customer, city 0 would get 1.2%, below the uniform 1.67%. So step 3’s ×8 underestimate is the filter happening to select the single hottest key. The step says exactly that and adds that a quieter city is slightly *over*estimated instead (recorded: city 30, 18,550 guessed, 13,323 real); `model.test.js` checks these shares and that most cities get less than a uniform share (51 of 60; 54 of 60 get fewer orders than DuckDB’s 18,550 guess).
4. **What helps.** Materialising product 0’s orders into a temporary table gives DuckDB an exact row count; it then builds on customers. The join on the stored table took 7.9 ms against 39.3 ms for DuckDB’s own plan in the recorded run, but that gap is not all the estimate’s doing: the stored table also spares the join from scanning and filtering all 1,000,000 orders. The fair reference is the right build side without storing (12.2 ms in step 4 of the same recording), so the bad guess cost about 27 ms and the pre-filtering saved about 4 ms more. The page now times and shows all three, and says which gap is which. Materialising took 30.5 ms, so the page says it pays only when the stored result is reused. For correlation there is no comparable fix in this version; the step names what other systems do (multi-column statistics such as PostgreSQL’s `CREATE STATISTICS`, sampling, adaptive re-planning) without claiming DuckDB does them.

Hash joins without indexes are fairly forgiving: the slowdowns here are two to three times, not the orders of magnitude that index nested-loop joins can suffer (Leis et al. 2015 discuss this). The page shows the size of the effect it measured rather than a dramatic one.

### Checks

- `tests/model.test.js` (Node): q-error and median; the Zipf-like masses sum to 1, follow k^−θ and match a seeded simulation of the generator’s transform; the expected counts reduce to the uniform-independent model at θ = ρ = 0; Cramér’s V is 0, ρ and 1 on constructed tables; the data SQL is deterministic and seeded; the SQL validator accepts reads and refuses writes, files and stacked statements; `parseProfile` on a real recorded DuckDB profile (`tests/fixtures/profile-best-province.json`); stable row keys; build-side and join-order change sentences.
- `tests/steps.html` (browser, the scripted check): loads the pinned engine, runs all five guided steps at both data sizes, evaluates each step’s claims (defined next to the step in `model.js`), measures the generated data against the model on a grid of settings, and records the card-preview frames. Its output is saved as `tests/steps.json`. In the recorded run (Chrome, Windows laptop, 2026-11-02) every claim passed at both sizes. A second full run after the copy fixes (same version, 2026-11-02T03:55Z) passed again; from it only the quiet-city count and the home-category shares were added to `steps.json` (marked with their own `recordedAt`), the rest of the file is the first run.
- `tests/steps.test.js` (Node): re-evaluates every step’s claims from the recorded estimates and actual rows in `steps.json`, recomputes the q-errors, checks the measured skew shares, filtered counts and Cramér’s V against `expected()` from the model (within 3% or six standard deviations), checks the recording used the pinned version, checks the quiet-city overestimate and the home-category share ρ + (1 − ρ)/12, and checks the card preview replays exactly the recorded frames, with the flip frame’s two times (DuckDB’s plan and the other build side) both taken from step 4’s run. `model.test.js` also checks the page copy: the lede and the preview’s description must not credit skew with the flip and must name the widened filter.
- `tests/make-preview.js` writes those frames into `model.js` from `steps.json`.

Run: `node projects/cardinal/tests/model.test.js && node projects/cardinal/tests/steps.test.js`. To re-record, open `/projects/cardinal/tests/steps.html`, copy `window.CARDINAL_STEPS` into `tests/steps.json`, run `make-preview.js`, then the tests.

### Simplified, or not shown

- One optimiser at one version. Other systems estimate differently (PostgreSQL multiplies single-table selectivities and keeps most-common-value lists, for example), and later DuckDB versions may change any of the behaviour above. If the pinned version changes, rerun `steps.html`; a failing claim means the step must change.
- Timings are from one browser tab, single-threaded WebAssembly, small data. The ratios, not the milliseconds, are the point, and even the ratios move with the machine. On the first run after the page loads, the wasm compiler is still optimising and timings are rougher.
- The scan of `orders` sometimes reports fewer real rows than it read, because DuckDB pushes min/max filters from a join’s build side into the scan at run time; the page shows those counts as DuckDB reports them.
- The card preview replays recorded numbers (it cannot run DuckDB). Its bars glide between recorded frames; every number it prints is from a recorded frame. Across the flip it labels the change as the filter widening and shows the other build side’s time beside DuckDB’s, since the city-scope time before the flip belongs to a different query.
- The look is a cyanotype engineering drawing: the optimiser's plan in white linework, and wherever a real count is 1.5 times or more off the guess, the guess struck through and the real figure and q-error written in red pencil (ringed past ×10). The red pencil is a visual cue only; the words "close", "too few" and "too many" carry the same information. Labels, figures and the red corrections use Inter 400/600, self-hosted from `@fontsource/inter@5.3.0` (Latin subset, SIL OFL 1.1, licence in `fonts/OFL.txt`); SQL stays in the system monospace.
- What would change the conclusion: a DuckDB version that keeps frequency statistics (it would see the skew), estimates correlated join filters jointly, or re-plans mid-query would close these particular gaps, and the steps would then have to show that instead.
