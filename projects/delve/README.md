# Delve

## What it’s for

A visitor plays a short solo dungeon crawl with dice and an oracle, and every roll shows its exact odds before and after it lands. The aim is that people learn by playing how two six-sided dice added together differ from one twelve-sided die (same chance to pass, very different ends), what advantage does to a d20, and why an oracle answer of “yes, but” feels fair: it sits on the faces just across the line from a “no”. Probability is meant to be felt, not lectured.

## How I know

**What is modelled.** Everything with a number in it lives in `model.js`, which runs in the browser and in Node.

- *Dice.* An expression is `NdM`, optionally exploding (`!`: a top face rolls again and adds), optionally keep-highest or keep-lowest (`khK`, `klK`; advantage is `2d20kh1`, disadvantage `2d20kl1`), plus or minus a constant. Sums are exact by repeated convolution. Keep-highest/lowest is exact by a dynamic programme over the faces from the best end: at each face value it chooses how many of the still-unassigned dice show that value (binomial count), and only the first K assigned count towards the sum. Where the number of equally likely outcomes fits in a double without rounding, the work is done in integer counts, so the page can say “21 of 36 ways” and the percentages are counts divided by a total. That includes exploding dice: a capped exploding die is counted as a chain of five rolls (`M^5` equally likely sequences), and a chain that stops after j + 1 rolls stands for `M^(4−j)` of them. The page shows “ways” only for plain dice; the exploding counts are used for the luck score.
- *Exploding is capped.* A die explodes at most four extra times. That cap is part of the rule (the roller obeys it too), so the distribution is finite and exact rather than truncated. Its effect is tiny: on a d6 the fifth roll in a chain happens once in 1,296 dice. The page says so where exploding dice appear (“at most four times”).
- *Checks.* Pass if the total meets the target. Beat it by 4 or more: strong (you gain a boon, +1 on the next check). Miss by 5 or more: disaster (the failure costs one more). These two thresholds are symmetric about the midpoint between “one short” and “just made it”. The four regions’ chances are summed from the exact distribution.
- *Oracle.* One d20. The likelihood sets a line: unlikely 15+, even 11+, likely 7+ for a yes. The two faces on either side of the line are the “but” answers, the top face is “yes, and”, the bottom face “no, and”. Band chances are counted face by face: unlikely 5 / 55 / 10 / 10 / 15 / 5 %, even 5 / 35 / 10 / 10 / 35 / 5 %, likely 5 / 15 / 10 / 10 / 55 / 5 % (no-and, no, no-but, yes-but, yes, yes-and). So yes overall is 30 %, 50 %, 70 %.
- *Dice generator.* sfc32, seeded through splitmix32. Faces come from rejection sampling on 32-bit outputs, so each face has exactly the same number of accepted raw values (no modulo bias).
- *Dungeon.* 8 to 12 rooms in 8 to 10 levels, gate first and the lowest hall last, one to three rooms per middle level. A run passes through one room per level, so it meets 8 to 10 rooms (fewer if health runs out). Doors join neighbouring levels by a monotone staircase, which guarantees every room has a way in and a way on and that corridors never cross. Rooms are drawn from 11 original situations without repeats. The map reveals a room only when you enter it; the doors from your room show as question marks.
- *Run rules.* Health 7, torches 6 (up to 7 with finds). Entering a room burns a torch; with none, d20 rolls lose a step (advantage becomes plain, plain becomes disadvantage) and other dice take one off. Careful failures cost a torch (health when there is no torch: 1 on a fail, 2 on a disaster), bold failures 2 health, clever failures 1. The cost line on each approach card is built by the same function the rules use (`costFor`), so in the dark the careful card says it costs health. Search once per room: the oracle answers “Is there anything useful here?” at the room’s likelihood. The warden in the last room must be beaten; each failure costs and you try again. Because every failure costs something and gains are bounded, every run ends.
- *Luck.* Each roll scores its percentile rank in its own distribution, P(X < x) + P(X = x)/2 (ties split), from 0 to 1, with mean exactly 1/2. The run’s luck is the sum, placed among all possible outcomes of the same rolls. It is exact when possible. A value with c of the D equally likely outcomes and b below it scores exactly (2b + c) / 2D; every score is put over one common denominator (the least common multiple of the 2D, about 3 × 10⁹ for the game’s dice), so run totals are whole numbers and ties are recognised exactly. The rolls are split into two halves, each half’s exact table is built, and the halves are merged in one sorted pass to give the share below and equal. If a half-table would pass 250,000 entries, the page instead runs 100,000 seeded simulated runs and says so, with a 99 % margin; over 300 random-choice runs that happened in 82 (27 %), and the luck sum took a median of 82 ms and at most 529 ms. An earlier version rounded scores to a 10⁻⁹ grid, which split some true ties; against exact fractions it was off by up to about 0.0002. The free oracle questions are flavour and do not count; searches do (a high face is a good find).

**What is simplified.** The dice are a pseudo-random generator, not physical dice; real dice are slightly imperfect. The game is tiny and its numbers (costs, targets, health and torch counts) were tuned by simulation so a sensible player usually wins (about 84 % when always taking the best odds, about 64 % when choosing at random, over 3,000 runs each) rather than derived from anything. The luck percentile asks “given these exact rolls were made, how lucky were the outcomes”, not “how lucky was the run including your choices”. The oracle is my own design; real solo-play oracles vary widely. The card preview uses a fixed seed (41) chosen so its four rolls show a mix of outcomes; the main page always starts from a random map seed, shown in the header. The dice have their own seed: “Same map again” keeps the map and draws fresh dice, so a replay cannot be memorised.

**What I checked, and how.** `node projects/delve/tests/model.test.js` (23 checks, about 10 s, all seeded):

- (a) Convolution and the keep DP match brute-force enumeration of every face sequence (exploding chains included) for 138 small expressions to within 10⁻¹², and integer counts match exactly; 2d6 ≥ 7 is 21/36, advantage ≥ 11 is 300/400, 4d6 keep 3 has mean 15869/1296. Eight larger expressions (10d20k5, 6d6!, 8d10kl3+2, 10d100, 3d12!k2, 5d20−4, 10d6, 4d8!kl2) agree with 200,000 seeded rolls of the real roller: values are pooled until each group expects at least 20 hits, and every group lies within a family-wise 99.9 % interval (Bonferroni). The parser accepts the documented forms and rejects malformed ones, including “2d6 adv” (advantage is one die rolled twice; it used to be read silently as “d6 adv”). Exploding dice’s whole-number counts sum to their total and match brute force.
- (b) d2, d4, d6, d8, d10, d12, d20 and d100 each pass a chi-square test over 600,000 rolls at α = 0.001; consecutive d6 pairs pass a 36-cell chi-square over 600,000 pairs. Generators with the same seed give the same stream; a saved state resumes identically; whole runs with the same seed and actions give identical journals.
- (c) Oracle bands sum to 1, are contiguous runs of faces, match the numbers stated above, and the “but” answers are exactly the two faces either side of the line. 300,000 asks per likelihood through the game itself pass a chi-square at α = 0.001.
- (d) For seeds 1 to 20,000: 8 to 12 rooms (and 8 to 10 levels deep), gate first, hall last, no repeated situation, every room reachable from the gate, every room except the hall has a door one level down, no crossing corridors. Every approach can both pass and fail with or without light and boons. 6,000 seeded runs under three policies (always first approach, random, best odds) all end, within 33 steps at most.
- Luck: the exact method equals brute force over every combination of a four-roll run (percentile and histogram); on three runs built to have many true ties (including an exploding 2d6), it equals an independent count with exact BigInt fractions to within 10⁻¹³ for both the share below and the share equal; simulation agrees with the exact answer within four standard errors; and a full run takes well under a second.
- Cards: for every approach, in light and in the dark, a seeded fail and disaster take exactly what the card says (128 pairs). Same map, different dice seed: identical dungeon, different rolls.
- (e) Every percentage in 400 simulated journals (6,049 of them) equals a fresh computation, by brute force where small. `app.js` holds no percentage, “N of M” or long decimal literal, and the page markup between the heading and the end of the stage contains no digits. In the browser, `DelveAudit()` recomputes the odds panel and every approach card from scratch; it agreed on every roll of a full run.

I checked the page in the desktop app’s browser pane at desktop width and at 375 × 812 (no horizontal scroll; scripted runs with `DelveAudit()` true before every roll; the “landed” label never crosses the target line or its label, and the ring stays clear of the axis numerals), the card preview at 320 × 200 and inside projects.html, with no console errors from this page. The pane did not run `requestAnimationFrame` for a hidden tab, so the preview loop was stepped with a timer shim; the paused still frame (the advantage roll landing on a strong pass, three rooms drawn) was checked directly, with every text on the card at 11 px or more (dice numerals included) and the ring inside the chart, and the frame rate was not measured.

**What would change the conclusion.** If the generator were biased the odds on screen would still be right but the rolls would not follow them; the chi-square tests are the guard. If the explosion cap were removed the charts would need a stated truncation. If someone reads “luckier than 85 %” as a statement about their decisions, it is not one: it only ranks outcomes of the rolls actually made.
