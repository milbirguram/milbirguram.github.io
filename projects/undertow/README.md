# Undertow

A seeded simulation of a retry storm: a short load spike, clients that time out and retry, and a service that stays down after the spike has gone. Page: `projects/undertow/index.html`. Model: `model.js` (browser and Node). Figures for many seeds: `data.js`, generated by `tools/build-data.js`. Tests: `tests/`.

## What it’s for

To let a visitor watch a metastable failure happen and undo it. A spike pushes waits past the clients’ timeout; the clients retry; the retries keep the queue full of work that nobody is waiting for, so the service stays down after the load has returned to normal. Nobody designed the collapse. It comes from two local rules, “give up after a timeout” and “try again”, each sensible on its own. The visitor then tries the standard remedies and finds that backoff and jitter, the usual first advice, do not free this system, while a retry budget, shedding old requests early, serving newest-first, or a circuit breaker do. The lesson is that reliability mechanisms, composed, can cause the outage they exist to prevent.

## How I know

**What is modelled.** Twenty clients share one Poisson stream of new requests at 28 a second (each request goes to a random client). A server has four workers and a queue of 200 places; service times are lognormal with mean 0.1 s (σ = 0.6 for the underlying normal), so capacity is about 40 a second and normal load is 70% of it. The network adds 0.1 s each way. Each attempt carries a client timeout (1 s by default) measured from sending; a reply counts as goodput only if it reaches the client before that deadline. A timed-out attempt is abandoned by its client but stays in the queue, and when it reaches a worker the worker serves it in full: the server cannot tell that nobody is waiting. That wasted work is tracked separately from useful work. A full queue rejects an arrival at once (the client hears after one network delay). A failed attempt (timeout, rejection or shed) may be retried under one of seven policies: none, immediate, fixed 0.5 s, exponential backoff (0.2 s base, doubling, 10 s cap), and backoff with full, equal or decorrelated jitter, following Brooker’s formulas; at most 3 attempts by default.

Remedies: a per-client retry budget (a token bucket; each new request earns 0.1 token, a retry costs 1, the bucket holds 1); server-side shedding at dequeue time of requests that have waited longer than the timeout, or longer than half of it; “newest first when behind” (serve the newest request while the oldest has waited over 0.25 s, a simple adaptive LIFO); and a per-client circuit breaker (opens for 2 s after 5 failures in its last 10 outcomes, then lets one probe through; while open, requests fail locally and are not sent). Triggers: a spike (offered load × factor for 4 s) or a server pause (workers stop for 2 s, and a request in service is held for the pause). It is a discrete-event simulation with an exact event heap; arrivals across a rate change use the memoryless restart, which is exact. Arrivals, service times and jitter draw from three separate seeded streams, so two runs with the same seed and different retry policies see the same arrivals.

“Stuck” is defined once, in `model.js`: goodput below 50% of offered load over the 10 s that begin 20 s after the trigger ends. “Recovered” on the page means goodput at 90% or more of offered load over a 2 s window, without later falling below 60%. Both the live “Recovered N s after” on the page and the many-seed medians count to the start of that window (the page calls the model’s own `recoveryTime` once the window is reached).

**What is simplified.** The most important simplification is that the load is open: new requests keep arriving at the same rate whatever the service does, as from many independent users. That is why backoff and jitter do not help here: they delay each retry but every request still makes up to three attempts, so the long-run load on the server is the same: in the collapse it settles at 3× the new requests, the attempt cap (the whole-run average is a little lower, about 2.9×, because it includes the healthy start). In a closed system, where each client waits for its reply before sending more, backoff also slows new work and helps more. There is one server, no load balancer, no autoscaling, no caching, and no cost to rejecting or shedding a request. The breaker and budget are simple versions of what real client libraries do; an adaptive concurrency limit is not modelled. Clients are identical. The queue is bounded at 200; the model also accepts an unbounded queue, which the page does not offer. Particles on the page fly for the model’s real network delay (0.1 s of simulated time), so they are brief streaks at higher speeds.

**What I checked, and how.** `node projects/undertow/tests/model.test.js` (about 10 s) and `node projects/undertow/tests/data.test.js` (about 30 s). The checks:

- (a) At low load with no trigger, goodput matches offered load within 2%, nothing is late or retried, and Little’s law holds at the server (time-average number present against arrival rate times mean time in system, within 3% at 30%, 50% and 70% load), as does the utilisation law. The lognormal service time has the stated mean.
- (b) For each of 20 seeds, the 2.5× spike with immediate retries keeps goodput under half of offered load in every 5 s window from 5 to 30 s after the spike, while offered load is back at its normal rate; the same seed without retries (identical arrivals) recovers within 15 s. With the same retries and no trigger, all 20 seeds stay healthy, so the collapse needs the trigger: it is a second stable state, not plain overload. A 2 s pause behaves the same way. In the collapse the workers are saturated and throughput is near capacity, but goodput is near zero.
- (c) Every claim the guided steps make is checked over 40 seeds and held in all 40 on the last run: healthy (40), no-retry recovers (40, median 9 s after the spike), immediate retries stuck (40), backoff stuck (40), full jitter stuck (40), retry budget switched on at 15 s recovers (40, median 18 s after switching). Backoff and jitter of every kind leave amplification above 2.3×. Shedding past the timeout stays stuck in all 10 seeds tested. Dropping requests that old is harmless, since their clients have already gone, but it is not enough: a request that has been at the server for more than about 0.8 s cannot get its reply back inside a 1 s timeout (0.1 s of network each way, plus about 0.1 s of service), and many from about 0.7 s miss too, so the requests it keeps, aged roughly 0.7 to 1 s, are still served for nobody, and over 90% of worker time stays wasted; shedding past half the timeout, LIFO and the breaker all recover. The challenge (3× for 6 s) is failed by immediate retries, backoff, jitter, stale shedding and 2 attempts, and passed (at least 18 of 20) by the budget (19 of 20), early shedding, LIFO and the breaker. The page’s figures for the steps and the phase map come from `data.js`, and `data.test.js` recomputes all of it from `model.js` and fails if anything differs. The challenge check on the page runs the visitor’s own settings over 20 seeds in the browser.
- (d) The same seed gives an identical run, a different seed a different one; stepping the model in small frame-sized increments gives the same events as one call; a longer run is the same run up to the shorter one’s end.
- (e) In every bin, goodput plus late completions equals throughput, so goodput never exceeds it. Busy time splits exactly into useful and wasted work and never exceeds what the workers had. Every request is open, succeeded or failed; every arrival at the server was rejected, shed, completed, or is still there. In the collapse over 70% of worker time is spent on abandoned requests; in health under 1%.

In the browser I checked the page at 1280 and 800 px wide and at 375 × 812, the `?preview` card at 520 × 325 and inside `projects.html`, the full guided path including switching on the budget mid-collapse, the challenge check, and the explore triggers. No console errors from this page.

**What would change the conclusion.** The tipping point depends on the margin between normal load and capacity, the queue length relative to the timeout, and the number of attempts. A timeout longer than the longest possible wait (a full queue of 200 at about 40 a second is 5 s, plus the network, so more than about 5.2 s here; the page’s slider stops at 4 s) stops timeout retries only while the queue never fills: a full queue rejects arrivals, and those rejections are retried whatever the timeout. With a 4 s timeout and immediate retries, the default 2.5× spike for 4 s recovers in 20 of 20 seeds (the queue peaks near 120), but 3× for 4 s recovers in 2 of 20, 2.5× for 6 s in 1 of 20, and the challenge (3× for 6 s) in none. A short queue (10 to 30 places) does not stop retries either: rejections are retried, and seed 1 makes over 300. What it stops is the queue holding a backlog of abandoned work, so nothing tips (10 of 10 seeds recover). A closed client population would make backoff more effective. The recover/stuck line in the phase map is drawn with a 4 s spike; a longer spike tips at a smaller size. The 1.4× to 1.6× columns are genuinely mixed: some seeds tip and some do not, which is what metastability looks like near the edge.

**How it looks, and what the look encodes.** The stage is drawn as a nautical chart: clients on a western shore, the queue a channel that narrows to its head, the workers at wharves. Retries are buoy-red triangles; first attempts are blue dots; a reply in time is a ringed dot. Two chart conventions carry data rather than decoration: the dashed contour across the channel is placed where the queue’s own ages pass the client timeout, and the italic soundings under it are the seconds the oldest request in each column has waited. The goodput chart is a tide gauge: the staff beside it shows the current goodput as a water level, with a pointer at the offered load.
